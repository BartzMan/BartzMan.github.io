Walk the roof.

A roofer's route, stop by stop

Every roof gets read the same way: up the ladder, then a path of boots across it. Tap a stop on the plan to see what a roofer looks at there.

Stop 1 of 5

Ridge

The ridge is the top line where two slopes meet. It is covered with cap shingles or a metal cap and takes the sun and wind first.

What a roofer looks atCaps seated flat, nothing lifted, and a straight line from end to end.
Stop 2 of 5

Vents

Pipes, vents and chimneys all poke through the roof, and each one is a spot water can find. They are sealed with flashing.

What a roofer looks atCracked pipe collars, lifted flashing edges and sealant that has dried out.
Stop 3 of 5

Valley

A valley is the inside corner where two slopes meet. Water from both slopes runs down it, so it works hardest in a storm.

What a roofer looks atFlashing that is tight and clean, underlayment lapped correctly, and no debris sitting in the channel.
Stop 4 of 5

Edges

The eaves and rakes are the outer edges. Wind gets under a roof here first, and the gutter line sits just below.

What a roofer looks atDrip edge in place, shingles or panels tight to the edge, and gutters that still carry water away.
Stop 5 of 5

Flat section

Low-slope and flat roofs drain slowly. A coating is rolled on to seal the surface and reflect the sun.

What a roofer looks atSeams, places where water sits after rain, and how worn the coating is.

Five roofs. One pair of boots.

Roofing, residential and commercial

Pick a roof type to see what it is, when you need it and what gets checked. Plain English, no jargon.

Shingle roof replacement

Asphalt shingles are the most common covering on a pitched home roof. A replacement strips the old layer, lays new underlayment on the deck, then new shingles.

You need it when shingles are curling, missing or worn through, or the roof is simply at the end of its life.

  • Underlayment goes down across the whole deck first
  • Ridge and edges are set tight so wind can't lift them
  • Old material comes off before new goes on

Metal roofing

Metal roofs run in long panels with raised seams that shed rain and snow quickly. They come in many colours, from charcoal to copper.

You need it when you want a roof that sheds snow and looks sharp, or on a home where the old roof has done its time.

  • Panels run eave to ridge in straight lines
  • Seams, flashing and trim at every edge
  • Fasteners and trim set in a consistent pattern

Tile roofing

Tile is a heavy, long-lasting roof covering with a classic terracotta look. Each tile overlaps the next, and the real waterproofing sits underneath.

You need it when tiles are cracked or slipped, or the layer under them has worn out.

  • Cracked or slipped tiles replaced one by one
  • Valleys and edges matter most, where tiles meet
  • The layer under the tile matters, not just the surface

Flat roof coatings

A coating is a white liquid layer rolled over a flat or low-slope roof. It seals small cracks and reflects sun so the roof stays cooler.

You need it when a flat roof is chalky, faded, or has hairline cracks but is still sound underneath. Common on commercial buildings.

  • Surface cleaned and seams checked first
  • Coating rolled on in even passes
  • Drains and edges need to stay clear

Roof repair

Repair fixes a leak, lifted flashing or a damaged patch without replacing the whole roof. It is the right call when the rest of the roof is still in good shape.

You need it when you see a stain on a ceiling, missing shingles after a storm, or water after rain.

  • The source of the leak matters, not just the stain
  • The damaged section is replaced and sealed
  • Sometimes repair is enough, sometimes replacement is
Useful on the ground

After Front Range hail.

Five things you can check from the yard, no ladder needed. Tap each eyelet as you go. Stay off the roof, and if anything looks wrong, call a roofer.

0 of 5 laced

  1. Gutters and vents

    Walk the house and look for dents or dings in gutters, downspouts and metal roof vents. Soft metal shows hail first.

  2. Granules in downspouts

    Look at the bottom of each downspout. A pile of loose gritty granules from shingles can mean the surface took a beating.

  3. Cracked tiles

    On a tile roof, scan from the ground for broken or shifted tiles, and look for fragments on the ground below.

  4. Dents on metal

    On a metal roof, look along the panels in low sun. Dents show up as shadows. Check the trim and flashing too.

  5. Leaks after the next rain

    Hail damage can hide. After the next rain, check ceilings and the attic for new stains, drips or damp insulation.
